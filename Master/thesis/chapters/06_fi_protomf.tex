\section{Prototype Grounding}
\label{sec:fi-why}
\vault{2026-07-11_2350_interactions-explained-by-item-features-taste-revealed}

An interaction occurs when the characteristics of an item match the taste of a
user, and matrix factorization models mirror that shape when they compute a
recommendation score as the product of a user embedding and an item embedding.
ProtoMF keeps this bilinear form but routes it through prototypes. Users and
items are represented by their similarity to a small set of learned prototype
vectors. Each item and user still owns a learned embedding. The prototypes
just act as the structural anchors of the embedding spaces.

Prototypes and embeddings are learned exclusively from the collaborative
signal. The collaborative signal records who interacted with what, and nothing
about what any of those things are, so no dimension of a representation
learned from it carries a meaning a human could read off. Most MF methods
share this opacity. To make its prototypes interpretable, ProtoMF names them
after training by inspecting each prototypes most associated items.
(Section~\ref{sec:bg-protomf}).
Post-hoc readings of opaque objects are problematic
(Section~\ref{sec:bg-iml-intrinsic}), and where possible, intrinsic methods
are preferred.

We aim to address these concerns by grounding the prototypes. Grounding, as
used in this thesis, means constraining the latent factors to a chosen
interpretable vocabulary. The model may only build its internal
representations from components that carry meaning on their own. The
vocabulary may well add information the model would not otherwise see, but we
impose it as a constraint for interpretability, not primarily as a
contribution to predictive power \textit{this sentence is uncertain might need
revision}. The approach of constraining a model's internals to expert
vocabulary has precedent outside of recommendation, where it was applied
likewise for interpretability with increased accuracy as a side
effect~\cite{niknazar2024sleep}.

A prototype grounded this way explains itself. Its meaning is its composition,
not a name assigned afterwards, and this property propagates to the score. A
recommendation becomes a match between the two ingredients, the user's
similarities to taste groups meeting the item's similarities to attribute
groups.

For the item ingredient, the appropriate vocabulary follows from how users
perceive items. To a user being shown an item with no prior external
knowledge, the item simply is the characteristics shown to them. The catalogue
records a coarse subset of these characteristics as attributes, which are in
the user's language even if they capture only part of what is perceived, a
limitation of the data rather than of the vocabulary. To the extent that such
attributes enter the model as interpretable features, they provide the
appropriate vocabulary to explain item prototypes as attribute groups.
Consider a clothing item's appearance as a characteristic, captured by a
photograph and described by recorded attributes like style, colour, and
pattern. It would be appropriate for the model to explain prototypes using
that attribute vocabulary, not, however, using filter activations of a CNN
layer, which would mean nothing to a shopper.

Explaining user prototypes as taste groups is not as straightforward. Users do
not state their taste, and in most cases would not be able to articulate it if
asked. Instead, users reveal their taste through their interactions. A CF user
embedding captures this, yet only as an unlabeled taste vector. User
attributes correlate with taste only loosely, are often thin, and may raise
privacy concerns when used for explanations. They therefore enter only as a
secondary mechanism (see Section~\ref{sec:fu-cold-users}). Interaction
histories must be the primary signal, adapted into an interpretable
vocabulary.

This asymmetry shapes the lineage of models in this thesis. The next section
therefore constructs the easier item side. The harder user side follows in
Chapter~\ref{ch:fu}, where its grounding routes through the item vocabulary
established here, and Chapter~\ref{ch:fufi} merges the two.

\section{The Model: Feature-Composed Item Factors}
\label{sec:fi-model}
\label{sec:fi-inventory}   % old inventory section parked here (block 3)
\vault{2026-06-16_1715_feature-injection-design-fork, 2026-07-11_1526_facet-a-claim-boundary-coldstart-2x2}
The last section already outlined how the model's item side is grounded in an interpretable vocabulary. But first, we will 
fix a few addtional requirements the design should deliever. The proposed model should:
\begin{itemize}
\item an extension of ProtoMF, not an additional model.
\item keep the ProtoMF paradigm, of recommendations being generated from a linear combination of prototype components.
\item stay or become even more intrinsically interpretable, as defined in section (reference section) with items attributes+prototypes being the core vocabulary.
\item make the attributes available to the model to leverage for predictive power.
\end{itemize}

We introduce our approach as feature-composed I-ProtoMF, in short
fI-ProtoMF. It differs from I-ProtoMF~\cite{melchiorre2022protomf} in
exactly one place. The host draws the embedding $\mathbf{t}$ of every
item from a lookup table with one free learned row per item. fI-ProtoMF
replaces this table by a composition in the manner of
LightFM~\cite{kula2015lightfm},
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{t} \,=\, \sum_{f \in \mathcal{F}_t} \mathbf{e}_f
\;\,\bigl(+\; \mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{ID}(t)}\bigr),
\label{eq:fi-composition}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{F}_t$ holds the feature values recorded for the item,
each $\mathbf{e}_f \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is a learned vector, and
$\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{ID}(t)}$ is an optional per-item row discussed
below. Everything downstream remains the host. The $L^t$ item prototypes
$\mathbf{p}^t_l$ stay free learned vectors, the item representation
$\mathbf{t}^* \in \mathbb{R}^{L^t}$ is still the vector of similarities
$\mathrm{sim}(\mathbf{t}, \mathbf{p}^t_l)$, the user is still a free
vector $\mathbf{u} \in \mathbb{R}^{L^t}$, and the score
$\text{I-score}(u,t) = \mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{t}^*$ is trained with the
host's loss and regularizers (Section~\ref{sec:bg-protomf}). The model
name keeps the f of feature rather than attribute because the
composition sums whatever encoded rows it is given, the ID row being the
first example, and choosing attribute-derived rows is what makes the
resulting model interpretable.
